For a new or very small roofing business, a website builder like Wix or Squarespace is a perfectly reasonable starting point. For a roofer who wants steady local enquiries from search, a custom build usually wins, because the things that bring in roofing jobs, such as area pages, speed, quote forms and photo galleries, are easier to do properly when nothing is holding you back. The honest answer depends on where your business is now and where you want it to be in two years.

## What a roofing website actually has to do

A roofing website has a narrower job than most people think. Someone with a leak, a slipping slate or an ageing roof searches on their phone, scans a few results and contacts one or two firms. You win the job by being found, looking credible, and making contact effortless.

That means the site needs to:

- Appear for searches in the towns you cover
- Load quickly on a phone
- Show real work and proof you are legitimate
- Offer a form, phone button and WhatsApp option within one tap
- Handle urgent enquiries differently from planned ones

Any platform can do some of this. The question is how easily, and what happens when you want more.

## The options compared

| | DIY builder (Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy) | Roofing template pack | Custom build |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | Very low | Low to moderate | Higher |
| Ongoing cost | Monthly subscription, rises with plan | Often a subscription or hosting fee | Hosting and optional maintenance |
| Setup time | Days, if you do it yourself | Days to a couple of weeks | Typically 2 to 4 weeks |
| Design flexibility | Within the template | Limited, shared with other roofers | Built around your business |
| SEO control | Basic to good | Often limited | Full control |
| Local area pages | Possible but fiddly at scale | Usually restricted | Designed in from the start |
| Speed | Variable, hard to tune | Variable | Can be tuned properly |
| Ownership | Tied to the platform | Often tied to the vendor | You own it |

None of these is automatically right or wrong. They suit different stages.

## Cost over time

Builders look cheap because the first month is cheap. Look at a three to five year horizon instead.

With a builder, you pay a recurring plan fee, possibly extra for a custom domain, email, booking tools or removing platform branding. You also pay with your own time: building pages, fixing layout problems, writing copy and working out why something does not look right on a phone. Hours spent on the website are hours not spent quoting or on a roof.

With template packs sold specifically to roofers, the upfront price is often attractive, but check what you actually own. Some are tied to the vendor's hosting, and moving away later can mean starting again. Ask before you buy whether you can take the site elsewhere.

A custom build costs more at the start. In return you own the site, choose your own hosting, and can add pages and features without asking a platform for permission. Over several years, the difference in total spend is often smaller than it first appears, and the site is generally doing more for you.

## SEO limits that matter to roofers

Builders handle the basics: page titles, descriptions, headings and a sitemap. For a roofer with one town and a few services, that may be enough.

The limits appear when you try to scale local visibility. Roofers typically want separate pages for each service (repairs, flat roofs, re-roofing, chimneys, guttering) and each area they cover. On a builder this can mean manually duplicating pages, with limited control over URL structure, technical markup and page speed. Template packs often make the same page for every town with only the name changed, which gives search engines little reason to rank them.

A custom site, especially on [WordPress](/wordpress-website-design/), lets you structure services and areas properly, add schema markup, control redirects and fix technical issues as they arise. Pair that with ongoing [SEO services](/seo-services/) and you have something you can grow rather than work around. No platform can promise rankings, but a flexible foundation removes the avoidable obstacles.

## Speed and mobile performance

Roofing enquiries come heavily from phones, often from someone looking at a ceiling stain. A slow page costs you that person.

Builders add their own scripts and layout code that you cannot remove. Sliders, animations and large images are easy to add and easy to get wrong. Template packs often ship with heavy themes and plugins. A custom build can be kept lean: compressed images, minimal scripts, and only the features you use. This does not guarantee a perfect score, but it gives you control over the result.

## Quote forms, galleries and emergency sections

These three features separate a roofing site that generates work from one that merely exists.

### Quote forms

A good roofing form is short: name, phone, postcode, type of problem, and an optional photo upload. Photos help you judge whether it is a repair or a larger job before you drive out. Many builders offer basic forms but struggle with photo upload, spam filtering, instant notifications or WhatsApp links without paid add-ons.

### Before and after galleries

Real photos of your own work are your strongest credibility signal. Group them by job type, add a short caption with the area and what was done, and compress them well. Builders often provide gallery blocks, but they can be slow to load and awkward to organise at scale. On a custom site, each gallery job can become its own page that supports your local SEO.

### Storm damage and emergency sections

After high winds or heavy rain, searches change. People want a fast response, not a brochure. A dedicated emergency section or page should include a prominent phone and WhatsApp button, what you do in an emergency (temporary repairs, tarping, making safe), your response area and, if true, your availability. Keep it factual and do not promise response times you cannot meet. You can also keep a separate page ready for insurance-related enquiries and explain how you document damage.

## When a builder is genuinely fine

We would rather tell you this than have you overspend. A builder is a sensible choice if:

- You are just starting and need something live this week
- Most of your work comes from referrals, repeat customers or a van and a Facebook page
- You cover one small area and a handful of services
- You do not plan to invest in search visibility yet
- You are happy to maintain it yourself

In that case, spend an afternoon making it clear: your services, your area, your phone number and WhatsApp, some real photos and your insurance details. That will beat a poorly managed expensive site.

## When a builder holds you back

It tends to become a constraint when:

- You want to rank in several towns and need many unique area pages
- You are paying for leads elsewhere and want your own site to carry more of the load
- Your site is slow and you cannot fix it
- You want integrations such as CRM, job management or custom notifications
- You have outgrown the template but changing it means rebuilding
- You want to own the asset instead of renting it

## How to choose

Ask yourself four questions.

1. **How do I get work today?** If it is almost entirely word of mouth, the website is a reference point. If you want search to bring in jobs, it is infrastructure.
2. **How many areas and services do I want pages for?** More than a handful points toward custom.
3. **How much of my own time can I give it?** A builder is not free if you are building and maintaining it yourself.
4. **Where do I want to be in two years?** Starting on a builder is fine, provided you know what moving off it would involve.
