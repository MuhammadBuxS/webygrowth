A dental website attracts more private patients when it answers the questions a nervous or high-value patient is silently asking: can I trust you, what will this involve, what will it cost, and how do I book? That means separate pages for each key treatment, honest information about fees and finance, a visible clinical team, and a booking or enquiry route that works on a phone. Good dental website design is mostly about reducing doubt, then making the next step easy.

## What private patients actually look for

Private patients, and especially those considering higher-value treatment, rarely choose on price alone. They tend to be weighing up several things at once:

- **Reassurance.** Many are anxious about dentistry in general. They look for a calm tone, clear explanations and signs that you will not judge or rush them.
- **Credibility.** Who will treat me, what are their qualifications, and how long have they been doing this?
- **Clarity on cost.** They want to know whether treatment is realistically within reach before they pick up the phone.
- **Evidence of quality.** Photos of the practice, the team and, where appropriate, results.
- **Convenience.** Opening hours, parking, accessibility and how quickly they can be seen.

If your site is mostly a list of services and a phone number, you are asking the patient to take all of that on trust. The sections below show how to close those gaps.

## Give each major treatment its own page

It is tempting to put implants, aligners, whitening and veneers on a single "Cosmetic and restorative" page. We would advise against it. Each treatment has a different patient, a different set of worries and a different search, so each deserves its own page.

| Treatment page | What the patient is worried about | What the page should cover |
|---|---|---|
| Dental implants | Pain, bone loss, longevity, cost | Who is suitable, the process step by step, healing time, who places them, aftercare, finance |
| Invisalign / clear aligners | Visibility, duration, results | How treatment works, typical timescales, retainers, suitability, consultation process |
| Teeth whitening | Safety, sensitivity, cost | Options available, what is realistic, who should not whiten, aftercare |
| Veneers | Natural look, irreversibility, cost | How they are planned, what the process involves, alternatives, longevity, maintenance |

Each page should follow a consistent structure so patients know where to look:

1. A plain-language summary of the treatment and who it suits.
2. The process, from consultation to completion, with an honest idea of how many visits it usually takes.
3. Cost information or a starting price, plus any finance options.
4. The clinician who would carry out the work, with a link to their profile.
5. Common questions, including pain, recovery, risks and alternatives.
6. A clear next step, such as a consultation booking.

## Be transparent about fees and finance

Cost is often what holds back an enquiry. Patients who cannot tell whether treatment is £500 or £5,000 will often not ask. You do not have to publish a rigid price list, but you can still help:

- Show a "from" price or a typical range for each treatment, with a sentence on what changes the figure.
- Explain what the consultation costs and what it includes.
- If you offer payment plans or finance, describe them in plain terms, including that finance is subject to status and that terms will be shared before any commitment.
- Be clear about what is and is not included, such as scans, follow-up visits or retainers.

Rules about how prices, offers and finance can be advertised exist, and they change. Check the current GDC advertising guidance and Advertising Standards Authority rules rather than relying on a blog post, including this one.

## Show the team and build credibility

People choose a person, not a building. A good team page includes:

- A professional photo of each clinician.
- Their role, qualifications and GDC registration number, presented accurately.
- A short, human introduction: areas of interest, how they like to work with nervous patients, perhaps something personal.
- Memberships or additional training that are real and current.

Add photos of the practice itself: reception, treatment rooms, the waiting area. Patients like to picture the visit before they make it. If you have genuine patient reviews, link to them or display them through a reputable review platform. Do not invent or edit them.

## Before-and-after images: use care

Before-and-after photos are among the most persuasive content on a dental site, and among the most sensitive. In general terms, you should expect to need:

- Clear, informed, written consent from the patient for this specific use.
- Images that are genuinely representative of what you offer and of the same patient, taken under comparable conditions, without misleading editing.
- Accompanying context, such as the treatment used and the timescale, rather than a bare image.
- A regular review of images you have published, so nothing stays up after consent is withdrawn.

The GDC publishes advertising guidance, and its expectations (and those of other bodies) can be updated. We are a web agency, not legal advisers, so please read the current GDC guidance and take advice from your indemnifier or regulator if you are unsure what you can publish.

## Make booking and enquiry easy

Interest fades fast if the next step is awkward. Aim to offer more than one route, because patients differ in how they like to get in touch.

- **Online booking or request form** for consultations, kept short: name, phone, email, treatment of interest, preferred time.
- **Click-to-call** on mobile, visible without scrolling.
- **email or messaging**, if your team can respond promptly and you have thought through data protection.
- **Clear confirmation** after submission: what happens next, and when they will hear from you.

Place the main call to action in the header, on every treatment page and at the end of long pages. Avoid pop-ups that cover the page on mobile. If you use a booking system, check how it behaves on a phone before you launch.

## Accessibility is part of patient care

A practice website should work for patients with visual, hearing, motor or cognitive needs, and for older patients who may struggle with small text. At a minimum:

- Sufficient colour contrast and readable text sizes.
- Descriptive alt text on meaningful images.
- Forms with proper labels and clear error messages.
- Navigation that works with a keyboard.
- Information about physical access to your premises, such as step-free entry, parking and accessible toilets.

Aim to meet the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) as a sensible benchmark. It helps real people and tends to improve the experience for everyone.

## Local SEO for dental practices

Most patients look for a dentist near them, so local search matters a great deal. The core pieces are the same as for any local service business:

- A complete, accurate Google Business Profile with the right categories, opening hours and photos.
- A steady stream of genuine patient reviews, with thoughtful replies. Be careful not to reveal patient information in public responses.
- Consistent name, address and phone details across your site and directories.
- Treatment and location content that is specific to your practice, rather than copied from elsewhere.
- Links from local sources, such as community organisations, local press or suppliers.

## Speed, mobile and technical foundations

Many patients search on a phone, often in a hurry. A slow site or one that is awkward on a small screen costs you enquiries.

- Compress images properly, particularly large photos of the practice and clinical work.
- Keep the page layout simple, with buttons large enough to tap.
- Avoid heavy sliders, auto-playing video and unnecessary plugins.
- Use HTTPS, and have a clear privacy notice and cookie approach that fits UK GDPR requirements.
